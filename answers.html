  1. Station 1: Home Exercise Prescription for Acute Lumbar Strain (Circuit 1, Slot 1)

    Content Areas: Professional Practice, Therapeutic Exercise

    Standardized Client Role Script

    You are Priya, a friendly but busy parent who wants a fast fix. When the candidate suggests exercises, ask 'Can I just go back to my hot yoga class this week instead? Isn't that basically the same thing?' If the candidate gives a clear, reasoned answer distinguishing gentle rehab exercise from a full yoga class at this stage, accept it and ask one follow-up about how many times a day to do the exercises. If the candidate is vague or just says 'sure, whatever feels okay,' push back once more: 'So it's fine if I just wing it?' React positively only to a candidate who gives a specific, graded, safe recommendation with a clear rationale.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Professional Practice: Confirms scope: recommends general therapeutic exercise within RMT training rather than diagnosing or prescribing a rehabilitation protocol reserved for another regulated profession
    • Professional Practice: Gives an honest, defensible answer about resuming hot yoga (advises against a full class until pain-free with controlled movement, rather than either forbidding it outright or approving it uncritically)
    • Therapeutic Exercise: Selects exercises appropriate for subacute lumbar strain (e.g. pelvic tilts, gentle knee-to-chest, cat-cow, transverse abdominis activation) rather than aggressive stretching or loaded movement
    • Therapeutic Exercise: Demonstrates or clearly describes correct form, including a stop sign for pain or symptom provocation
    • Therapeutic Exercise: Specifies a clear, realistic dosage (repetitions, sets, frequency per day)
    • Communication: Uses plain, jargon-free language and checks the client's understanding before ending the station
    • Overall Performance: Demonstrates confident, client-centred clinical reasoning throughout the exchange

    Model Answer

    The candidate acknowledges Priya's progress and explains that at 10 days post-strain, with pain still present at rest transitions, the tissue is in an early remodelling phase that benefits from controlled, low-load movement rather than the deep stretching and heat exposure of a full hot yoga class. The candidate recommends 2-3 exercises appropriate to this stage: pelvic tilts, gentle supine knee-to-chest (one leg at a time, avoiding end-range if it reproduces pain), and cat-cow through a pain-free range, each demonstrated with clear cueing on breath and pace. Dosage is specific: 8-10 repetitions, 2 sets, once or twice daily, stopping short of any sharp or radiating pain. When asked about hot yoga, the candidate gives a clear, honest answer: holding off on the full class for another week or two and reassessing at the next visit, since sustained end-range postures in a heated room could aggravate a still-healing strain, while reassuring Priya this is a short-term modification, not a long-term restriction. The candidate stays within RMT scope, framing this as general movement guidance rather than a medical prescription, and invites Priya to report any increase in symptoms. The candidate closes by confirming Priya can repeat the exercises back or demonstrate them, ensuring genuine understanding rather than passive nodding.

  2. Station 2: Initial History for Suspected Disc-Related Low Back Pain (Circuit 1, Slot 2)

    Content Areas: Professional Practice, Client History

    Standardized Client Role Script

    You are Marcus, a warehouse worker with a 3-year history of intermittent low back pain that has recently started radiating into his left calf with occasional numbness in his foot. Mention this radiation and numbness only if asked directly about leg symptoms. If asked about bowel or bladder changes, saddle numbness, or unexplained weight loss, answer no to all. If the candidate never asks about red flags (bowel/bladder, saddle anesthesia, unexplained weight loss, fever, trauma), do not volunteer them, and appear mildly impatient if the interview drags without ever getting to 'what are you going to do about it.' Reward a candidate who explains why they are asking sensitive questions before asking them.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Professional Practice: Obtains clear informed consent for the interview and explains confidentiality before starting sensitive questioning
    • Client History: Establishes onset, mechanism, duration, and progression of symptoms
    • Client History: Characterizes the radiating leg symptom (location, quality, whether it follows a dermatomal pattern, aggravating/easing factors)
    • Client History: Screens for red flags: bowel/bladder changes, saddle anesthesia, unexplained weight loss, fever, night pain unrelieved by rest, history of cancer, significant trauma
    • Client History: Asks about prior treatment, medications, and relevant medical history
    • Professional Practice: Correctly reasons that physical assessment can proceed today since no red flags are present, or correctly identifies that any positive red flag would require referral before treatment
    • Communication: Uses open-ended questions first, then narrows with specific follow-ups, and explains clinical reasoning to the client in plain language
    • Overall Performance: Conducts a systematic, unhurried interview that a real client would experience as thorough and respectful

    Model Answer

    The candidate begins by explaining the purpose of the intake, confirming Marcus's understanding that information shared is confidential and used to guide safe, effective treatment, and obtaining verbal consent before proceeding. The history opens broadly (how long has this been going on, what does a typical day of pain look like) before narrowing to the radiating symptom: when the leg symptoms started, exactly where they travel, whether they follow a specific pathway down the leg, and what makes them better or worse, such as sitting, coughing, or forward bending. The candidate explicitly and sensitively screens for red flags, briefly explaining why ("I ask everyone with back pain these questions to rule out anything that needs urgent medical attention") before asking about bowel or bladder changes, numbness in the groin or inner thighs, unexplained weight loss, fever, and any recent significant trauma. The candidate also asks about prior treatment for this issue, current medications, and other relevant medical history. Having found no red flags, the candidate clearly states to Marcus that based on this history it is appropriate to proceed with a physical assessment today, and explains what that assessment will involve before moving forward, again checking for consent. Throughout, the candidate paces the interview so it feels conversational rather than like a checklist, and periodically summarizes what Marcus has said to confirm accuracy.

  3. Station 3: Assessment for Suspected Sacroiliac Joint Dysfunction (Circuit 1, Slot 3)

    Content Areas: Assessment

    Standardized Client Role Script

    You are Denise, cooperative and athletic, motivated to get back to running. Answer assessment questions accurately based on the pain description given (localized to the right PSIS/buttock, aggravated by single-leg loading). When the candidate performs standing flexion test, report the right side moving first/further if asked in a way consistent with SIJ dysfunction. When asked to point with one finger to the worst spot of pain, point just below and medial to the right PSIS (Fortin's finger sign pattern). Report pain reproduction with any SIJ provocation-style test performed correctly (e.g. compression, distraction, thigh thrust, Gaenslen's) but say a straight leg raise does not reproduce your usual pain and produces only mild hamstring tightness. If the candidate never asks you to localize the pain with one finger or never performs any SIJ-region orthopedic test, do not volunteer this information.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Assessment: Observes posture and gait, including pelvic level and any asymmetry, before hands-on testing
    • Assessment: Performs active lumbar range of motion and notes whether it reproduces the client's specific pain
    • Assessment: Performs at least two relevant SIJ provocation or positional tests (e.g. standing flexion test, thigh thrust, compression, distraction, Gaenslen's test, Fortin's finger test) with correct technique
    • Assessment: Performs a neurological screen or straight leg raise to help rule out a lumbar disc/nerve root source
    • Assessment: Palpates relevant structures (PSIS, sacral sulcus, gluteal and piriformis region, lumbar paraspinals) and correlates findings with reported symptoms
    • Assessment: Synthesizes findings into a clear clinical impression distinguishing likely SIJ-region involvement from a lumbar spine source
    • Communication: Explains each test to the client before performing it and checks in about pain during testing
    • Overall Performance: Conducts an efficient, logical, safety-conscious assessment sequence within the time allowed

    Model Answer

    The candidate begins with observation, checking pelvic height, gait pattern, and any visible asymmetry, then asks Denise to actively move through lumbar flexion, extension, and side bending, noting whether any motion reproduces her specific pain versus general stiffness. The candidate then asks Denise to point with one finger to the worst area of pain, correctly interpreting a positive finding just below the PSIS as suggestive of SIJ involvement. The candidate performs the standing flexion test bilaterally, correctly identifying earlier or greater movement of the right PSIS as a positive finding, then performs at least two further SIJ provocation tests such as thigh thrust and sacral compression/distraction, explaining each before performing it and confirming whether it reproduces Denise's usual pain. To help rule out a lumbar disc source, the candidate performs a straight leg raise and a brief neurological screen (dermatomes, myotomes, or reflexes as appropriate), noting the absence of radiation past the knee and a negative or non-reproducing straight leg raise. The candidate palpates the PSIS, sacral sulcus, and surrounding gluteal musculature bilaterally for tenderness or asymmetry. The candidate concludes by summarizing the clinical picture to Denise in plain language: findings are most consistent with right SIJ region dysfunction rather than a lumbar disc or nerve root problem, based on the localized pain, positive provocation tests, and negative neurological findings, while noting this is a working impression rather than a diagnosis.

  4. Station 4: Assessment Modifications for an Elderly Client with Osteoporosis (Circuit 1, Slot 4)

    Content Areas: Professional Practice, Assessment

    Standardized Client Role Script

    You are Helen, polite but a little anxious about being touched or moved because a friend her age recently fractured a vertebra. If the candidate does not explain why they are modifying their approach, ask directly: 'Is it safe for you to press on my back? I'm worried about my bones.' Respond well to a candidate who explains clearly what they will avoid and why, and who checks pressure tolerance as they go. If a candidate performs or describes a forceful spinal thrust, high-velocity technique, or aggressive end-range range of motion testing, say 'that feels like a lot of pressure, please be gentle' and look uncomfortable.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Professional Practice: Obtains informed consent that specifically addresses the planned assessment and the modifications being made for osteoporosis
    • Professional Practice: Explains contraindicated or cautioned techniques in plain language (e.g. no forceful spinal compression, no high-velocity thrust techniques, caution with aggressive end-range spinal motion) consistent with the physician's referral note
    • Assessment: Performs active range of motion assessment gently, staying within a pain-free and comfortable range rather than pushing to end range
    • Assessment: Avoids or clearly modifies any test involving forceful spinal loading, compression, or high-velocity movement
    • Assessment: Palpates using appropriately graded pressure and checks in verbally throughout
    • Assessment: Screens for signs that might suggest an undiagnosed vertebral compression fracture (e.g. new onset severe localized pain, point tenderness over a specific vertebra, recent fall or trauma)
    • Communication: Checks in verbally throughout testing, adjusting immediately if the client expresses discomfort
    • Overall Performance: Demonstrates sound clinical judgment in balancing a thorough assessment against the client's specific medical vulnerability

    Model Answer

    The candidate begins by reviewing the referral note with Helen, confirming her diagnosis and any recent changes, and explicitly asking whether she has had any falls, new severe pain, or a recent change in symptoms that might suggest a new fracture, all of which she denies. The candidate then explains, before touching her, that the assessment will be gentle and will avoid any forceful pressure, high-velocity movement, or pushing her spine to a hard end range, directly addressing her stated worry about her bones. Active range of motion is assessed with Helen moving herself through flexion, extension, and side bending only as far as is comfortable, with the candidate observing rather than passively overpressing at end range. Palpation is performed with light to moderate graded pressure, checking in verbally ("how does that feel, let me know if it's too much") and adjusting immediately based on her feedback. The candidate avoids any spinal compression or high-velocity technique consistent with the physician's note, and specifically screens for point tenderness over an individual vertebra or any sign suggestive of an undiagnosed compression fracture, finding none. The candidate closes by summarizing that findings are consistent with generalized chronic stiffness rather than anything acute or concerning, reassures Helen about the plan for gentle, safe treatment, and confirms she feels comfortable proceeding at a future visit.

  5. Station 5: Treatment Consent and Technique Selection for Acute Lumbar Strain (Circuit 1, Slot 5)

    Content Areas: Professional Practice, Treatment

    Standardized Client Role Script

    You are Aaron, in noticeable discomfort and a bit nervous about being touched since it still hurts. If the candidate does not explain draping and consent clearly before starting, ask 'What exactly are you going to do, and what do I need to take off?' If the candidate proposes deep, aggressive tissue work on the acutely strained muscle itself, wince and say 'that's really sharp, can you go lighter?' Respond well to a candidate who explains a graded approach (working around the acute area first, lighter pressure directly on it) and who re-confirms consent before changing technique or pressure.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Professional Practice: Obtains specific informed consent for the treatment plan, including areas to be treated and general technique/pressure to be used
    • Professional Practice: Explains draping procedure clearly and maintains proper draping throughout, exposing only the area being treated
    • Treatment: Selects techniques appropriate for an acute strain (e.g. gentle effleurage, light pressure, addressing surrounding compensatory tension rather than aggressive direct work on the acutely strained tissue)
    • Treatment: Avoids deep, provocative, or high-velocity techniques directly over the acutely injured area
    • Treatment: Grades pressure appropriately and checks in verbally throughout, adjusting based on client feedback
    • Treatment: Positions the client appropriately (e.g. supportive pillowing) to minimize strain during treatment
    • Communication: Re-confirms consent before changing pressure, technique, or area treated
    • Overall Performance: Demonstrates safe, client-centred clinical decision-making appropriate to the acute stage of healing

    Model Answer

    The candidate explains the treatment plan to Aaron before he undresses: general full-body or regional draping with only the low back exposed as needed, a gentle approach given how recent and acute his strain is, and an invitation to speak up at any time if something is too much. Consent is obtained specifically for this plan, not just a general nod. During treatment, the candidate begins with light effleurage over the broader lumbar and gluteal region to assess tolerance and encourage general relaxation before working anywhere near the acute strain site, explaining this sequencing to Aaron as they go. Direct work over the strained erector spinae itself is kept light, using techniques such as gentle broadening strokes rather than deep, sustained, or provocative pressure, since aggressive work on an acutely inflamed muscle can increase irritation. The candidate checks in verbally at regular intervals ("how's that pressure, still okay?") and immediately backs off when Aaron reports sharp discomfort, re-confirming he is comfortable continuing at a lighter pressure before proceeding. Draping is maintained appropriately throughout, with only the treatment area exposed. The candidate closes the session by briefly summarizing what was done and setting expectations for how Aaron might feel afterward, checking that he has no further questions before he gets up.

  6. Station 6: Managing a Boundary Issue During Chronic Low Back Treatment (Circuit 1, Slot 6)

    Content Areas: Professional Practice, Treatment

    Standardized Client Role Script

    Partway through the massage, once the candidate is working on your lower back, make an unsolicited personal comment that is clearly outside appropriate therapist-client boundaries (for example, commenting on the candidate's appearance in a flirtatious way, or asking them out for a drink after the session). Watch how they respond. If the candidate firmly, calmly, and professionally redirects the conversation back to treatment and clearly restates the professional nature of the relationship without being harsh or ending the session unnecessarily, apologize and say 'sorry, that was inappropriate, let's get back to it' and continue cooperating normally for the rest of the station. If the candidate ignores the comment, laughs it off in a way that seems to accept it, or reciprocates any personal interest, continue pushing the boundary further with a second comment.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Professional Practice: Responds to the boundary-crossing comment promptly, calmly, and without escalating conflict
    • Professional Practice: Clearly and firmly restates the professional nature of the therapeutic relationship
    • Professional Practice: Does not ignore, laugh off, or reciprocate the inappropriate comment
    • Professional Practice: Makes a reasoned decision about whether it is appropriate to continue the session after addressing the boundary, and documents the incident appropriately if ending or continuing
    • Treatment: Continues to deliver appropriate, effective techniques for chronic mechanical low back pain (e.g. sustained pressure, myofascial or trigger point work to paraspinals, gluteals, hip flexors) once the boundary issue is addressed
    • Treatment: Maintains appropriate draping and professional physical conduct throughout, especially after the incident
    • Communication: Uses clear, non-judgmental, professional language to set the boundary rather than an accusatory or overly apologetic tone
    • Overall Performance: Maintains composure and professionalism throughout the remainder of the session

    Model Answer

    When Gary makes the inappropriate comment, the candidate pauses briefly, maintains a calm and even tone, and directly but respectfully addresses it: for example, 'I want to keep our sessions focused on your treatment, so let's keep things professional.' The candidate does not laugh it off, ignore it, or respond with any personal information or reciprocal interest, and does not become visibly flustered or punitive in tone. Having clearly restated the boundary, the candidate briefly assesses whether it is appropriate to continue: since Gary immediately apologizes and there is an established, otherwise appropriate treatment history, the candidate reasonably decides to continue the session while remaining alert for any repeat behaviour, rather than either ignoring the incident entirely or ending the session unnecessarily. The candidate resumes appropriate treatment for Gary's chronic mechanical low back pain, using sustained, targeted pressure to the bilateral lumbar paraspinals, gluteal musculature, and hip flexors, checking in on pressure tolerance as usual. The candidate maintains clear, professional physical conduct and draping for the remainder of the session. After the session, the candidate notes mentally (and would document in the chart) that the boundary comment occurred, how it was addressed, and the client's response, in case it recurs or needs to be discussed with a supervisor or the College.

  7. Station 7: Postpartum Low Back Pain: Treatment, Exercise, and Documentation (Circuit 1, Slot 7)

    Content Areas: Professional Practice, Treatment, Therapeutic Exercise

    Standardized Client Role Script

    You are Sofia, a new parent, tired, and eager for relief but cautious because she has heard conflicting advice about what exercises are safe with diastasis recti. Ask the candidate directly: 'Is it safe for me to do crunches or planks to strengthen my core?' Respond well to a candidate who correctly advises against traditional crunches/planks in early postpartum recovery with diastasis recti and instead suggests gentle, safe alternatives, and who explains this within their scope (general movement guidance, not a rehab diagnosis). If a candidate gives no exercise reasoning at all, or greenlights crunches/planks without qualification, ask a skeptical follow-up: 'Are you sure that's okay?'

    Examiner Checklist

    • Professional Practice: Obtains informed consent for today's specific treatment plan and confirms comfort with positioning given the postpartum context
    • Treatment: Selects appropriate hands-on techniques for chronic postpartum lumbar/SI region tension (e.g. soft tissue work to lumbar paraspinals, gluteals, and hip region) with sensible pressure and positioning
    • Therapeutic Exercise: Correctly advises against traditional crunches and planks given the noted diastasis recti and early postpartum stage
    • Therapeutic Exercise: Recommends safe, appropriate alternative exercises (e.g. gentle pelvic floor/transverse abdominis activation, pelvic tilts, supported bridging) with correct form and reasonable dosage
    • Professional Practice: Frames exercise guidance within RMT scope, distinguishing general movement advice from a postpartum rehabilitation diagnosis or program reserved for another provider
    • Professional Practice: Clearly explains to the client what will be documented from today's visit using the SOAP structure: what she reported, what was objectively found, the therapist's clinical assessment/impression, and the plan for next visit
    • Communication: Communicates with warmth and practicality appropriate to a tired new parent, avoiding jargon
    • Overall Performance: Integrates treatment, exercise prescription, and documentation into one coherent, well-paced visit

    Model Answer

    The candidate begins by checking in on how Sofia has felt since the last visit and confirming consent for today's plan: continued soft tissue work to the lumbar paraspinals, gluteal region, and hips, with positioning adjusted for her comfort as a postpartum client (for example, extra pillow support). Hands-on treatment addresses the chronic dull ache with graded, sustained pressure to the identified areas, checking in on pressure tolerance throughout. When Sofia asks about crunches and planks, the candidate correctly and confidently explains that with a noted 2-finger diastasis recti at only 8 weeks postpartum, traditional crunches and planks place excessive intra-abdominal pressure on a still-recovering linea alba and are not recommended yet, without alarming her. The candidate instead recommends a gentle, appropriate alternative such as diaphragmatic breathing paired with a gentle pelvic floor and transverse abdominis activation (drawing the lower belly in gently on exhale) and posterior pelvic tilts, demonstrating correct form and suggesting a low starting dosage such as 5-10 repetitions once or twice daily, reassessing as she progresses. The candidate is clear that this is general movement guidance within RMT scope rather than a postpartum rehabilitation program, and mentions that a pelvic health physiotherapist could offer more specific core and pelvic floor rehabilitation if Sofia wants a deeper program. Before ending, the candidate explains plainly what will go in today's chart note using the SOAP structure: what Sofia reported (persistent dull lumbar/SI ache since delivery), what was objectively found and treated (palpable tension in the lumbar paraspinals, gluteals, and hips, addressed with soft tissue work), the candidate's own clinical impression that this reflects chronic postpartum musculoskeletal strain from delivery and altered posture and core function, and the plan for the next visit including the home exercises given, checking that she has no other questions.

  1. Station 8: Exercise Guidance and Scope Boundaries for Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy (Circuit 2, Slot 1)

    Content Areas: Professional Practice, Therapeutic Exercise

    Standardized Client Role Script

    You are Elias, a construction supervisor who wants clear answers. Ask directly: 'So what's actually torn in there, and how many weeks until I'm back to normal?' If the candidate stays within scope, clarifies they cannot diagnose a specific structural finding or guarantee a timeline, and instead describes the general nature of tendinopathy and realistic expectations, accept it. If the candidate confidently states a specific diagnosis (e.g. 'you have a partial tear of the supraspinatus') or promises an exact recovery date, look confused and ask 'wait, I thought only a doctor could tell me that.'

    Examiner Checklist

    • Professional Practice: Declines to state a specific structural diagnosis, correctly deferring to the referring physician's diagnosis and scope
    • Professional Practice: Avoids promising a specific recovery timeline, instead giving a realistic, honest general expectation
    • Therapeutic Exercise: Selects exercises appropriate for subacute rotator cuff tendinopathy (e.g. pendulum swings, scapular retraction, isometric external/internal rotation) rather than aggressive overhead loading
    • Therapeutic Exercise: Avoids recommending exercises that reproduce impingement (e.g. full overhead press, aggressive overhead stretching) at this stage
    • Therapeutic Exercise: Demonstrates or clearly describes correct form and gives a specific, reasonable dosage
    • Communication: Explains the general nature of tendinopathy and healing timelines in plain, honest language without overstepping scope
    • Overall Performance: Balances honesty and reassurance while clearly respecting professional and legal scope limits

    Model Answer

    The candidate acknowledges Elias's frustration and explains clearly that a specific structural diagnosis, such as whether a tendon is partially torn, is outside RMT scope and was already provided by his physician as rotator cuff tendinopathy; the candidate is happy to explain what that general term means (irritation and breakdown of the tendon from repetitive overhead loading) without contradicting or restating the physician's specific diagnosis. On timeline, the candidate avoids promising an exact number of weeks, instead honestly explaining that tendinopathy recovery is variable, often taking several weeks to a few months depending on load management and consistency with exercise, and that progress will be reassessed at each visit. The candidate then teaches 2-3 exercises appropriate for this subacute stage: pendulum swings for gentle mobility, scapular retraction (squeezing the shoulder blades together) for postural support, and isometric external rotation against light resistance (such as a rolled towel against the side) to load the cuff without provoking impingement. The candidate explicitly avoids prescribing full overhead pressing or aggressive overhead stretching, explaining that these movements can currently reproduce his impingement pain. Dosage is specific: isometrics held 5-10 seconds for 5-8 repetitions, pendulums for 30-60 seconds, once or twice daily. The candidate closes by encouraging Elias to modify or stop any exercise that reproduces sharp pain and to raise concerns with his physician if progress stalls.

  3. Station 10: Assessment for Suspected Rotator Cuff Tear (Circuit 2, Slot 3)

    Content Areas: Assessment

    Standardized Client Role Script

    You are Wendell, cooperative but frustrated by the weakness. When the candidate performs the empty can test (resisted abduction with thumbs down), report significant weakness and pain and let your arm give way slightly if prompted realistically. When the candidate performs the drop arm test (lowering the arm slowly from full abduction), report that you cannot control the lowering smoothly past about 90 degrees and it drops suddenly. When the candidate performs a lift-off test or resisted internal rotation behind the back, report pain and weakness there too if asked. If the candidate does not distinguish active from passive range of motion, do not volunteer this distinction.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Assessment: Observes posture, shoulder symmetry, and any visible muscle atrophy before hands-on testing
    • Assessment: Assesses and explicitly compares active versus passive range of motion, correctly noting a discrepancy (limited/weak active, near-full passive) as significant
    • Assessment: Performs the empty can test (Jobe's test) with correct technique and interprets weakness/pain as a positive supraspinatus finding
    • Assessment: Performs the drop arm test with correct technique and interprets an inability to smoothly lower the arm as a positive finding suggestive of a significant cuff tear
    • Assessment: Performs at least one additional relevant cuff test (e.g. lift-off test or resisted external/internal rotation) to assess other cuff muscles
    • Assessment: Performs a basic neurovascular screen given the traumatic mechanism
    • Assessment: Synthesizes findings into a clear clinical impression suggestive of a possible rotator cuff tear and appropriately notes the need for physician follow-up or imaging
    • Communication: Explains each test before performing it and monitors for pain throughout
    • Overall Performance: Conducts a safe, systematic, time-efficient assessment appropriate to a recent traumatic injury

    Model Answer

    The candidate begins with observation, checking for muscle atrophy, symmetry, and resting posture of the shoulder girdle, then explicitly compares active and passive range of motion, correctly identifying that Wendell can achieve near-full passive motion but has significantly limited and weak active motion, a pattern that raises suspicion for a rotator cuff tear rather than a primarily capsular restriction. The candidate performs the empty can test, explaining it beforehand, and correctly interprets significant weakness and pain as a positive finding suggestive of supraspinatus involvement. The candidate then performs the drop arm test, correctly interpreting Wendell's inability to smoothly lower his arm past roughly 90 degrees as a positive finding that raises concern for a more significant tear. A further test such as the lift-off test or resisted internal/external rotation is performed to assess subscapularis and other cuff musculature, again finding weakness and pain. Given the traumatic mechanism, the candidate performs a brief neurovascular screen of the arm and hand. The candidate synthesizes these findings into a clear impression: the combination of a traumatic mechanism, active-passive range of motion discrepancy, and multiple positive cuff tests is consistent with a possible rotator cuff tear, and the candidate appropriately notes that this should be communicated back to the referring physician, and that further imaging may already be indicated given the mechanism and findings, rather than proceeding to aggressive treatment without that context.
